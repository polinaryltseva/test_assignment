PART 1 · FRAMING

Goal & metric

DECISION: RECURRING REVENUE / MARGIN GUARDRAIL

The business goal is recurring revenue. Every alternative breaks on its own: users and activity can be bought with trials, retention rises with zero acquisition, revenue-per-customer rises when cheap customers are lost. Revenue contains all drivers — improving one at another's expense shows up immediately.

The guardrail is margin per paying customer: every generation costs real compute, video far more than an image, so revenue can be bought at a loss.

Revenue this month = Σ over cohorts [ size × share still active × average payment ] — and it moves as:

Revenuet = Revenuet−1 × NRR + New + Recovered,  NRR = (1 − churn) + upgrades − downgrades

driverbehaviourshare of revenuecall
Acquisitionadditive50%out of scope — the unpaid funnel is not in the data
Retentioncompounds monthly28%focus 1
Expansioncompounds on survivors19%focus 2
Recoveryadditive, small3%too small

New revenue dominating means the business currently runs on acquisition; retained and expanded dollars multiply the whole base every month, which is why they are the levers even at smaller shares.

Retention & expansion — final definitions and what the analysis found

Retention. Renewal is measured on 30-day billing cycles (grid shifted 2 days so short calendar months land right), no grace period — rebills pile at days 30–32 and the distribution has no gap for one to sit in. A renewal is a non-reactivation subscription charge inside the cycle; win-backs (median day 64) are recovered revenue, not retention. Annual is excluded — 87.5% pay exactly once, so a monthly renewal is undefined for them. Result: first renewal 51%, then 69–81% — one cliff, then a plateau. The first 30 days carry the highest churn, the largest sample, and are earliest to act on.

Expansion. Two routes, treated separately: subscription upgrades ($211k, n=3,306, ladder Basic < Pro < Ultimate < Creator) and credit top-ups ($195k, n=6,773, plan-independent). Both happen early — medians inside the first ~3 weeks, Basic upgrades same-day (wrong plan at signup). Result: expansion follows month-1 volume and ladder position, and behavior predicts it 4–6× more strongly than it predicts renewal.

Why these CJM steps. The paid journey is pay → first generation → week-1 habit → month-1 habit → (expansion) → first renewal → stay. Week 1 is the early signal — fast to measure, and ≥3 active days is the first threshold with materially higher subsequent engagement. Month 1 is the path to the renewal at ~day 30 — days and gaps over the full month predict it better than week 1 alone. Effectively all of the retention uplift available in this data is concentrated in the first 30 days, which is why every activation and habit bet below targets W1 and M1, not later cycles.

PART 2 · LTV MODELING

Churn, LTV, and the cost ceiling

DECISION: LTV $117, QUOTED AS $115–203

Churn is defined on billing cycles, not calendar months. Rebills land at days 30–32 (41.3pp of all renewals), there is no gap for a grace period to sit in, so the cycle boundary is the deadline. Churned at cycle m = no scheduled charge in cycle m, measured only on accounts whose cycle m fully elapsed; only 2.6% ever miss a cycle and pay again, so the strict rule isn't counting dunning gaps. Win-backs (median day 64, 4.7% of revenue) are recovered revenue, not retention. Annual is excluded from churn: 87.5% of annual accounts pay exactly once, so a monthly renewal is undefined for them — a blended number would credit retention nobody observed.

Rejected: the dashboard formula 1 − A(t+1)/A(t) — negative in four of five months while real churn sat at 43–51%; it tracks acquisition. Chained renewal rates on at-risk denominators handle censoring with no survival model in between.

REVENUE RETENTION BY CYCLE TRANSITION — expansion softens churn, never out-runs it
NRR never crosses 100% with win-backs excluded → upsell cannot substitute for fixing the first bill. Also: of accounts that renewed once, only 46% generated anything in the cycle they just paid for — $89k, 36% of cycle-1 revenue, is dormant. An early-warning list, and a refund liability sitting inside GRR.

The argument to have is about the tail, not the discount rate.
DEFINITIONS — GRR & NRR, THE MATH

Take B = the subscription revenue of the accounts entering a cycle. Over the transition three things can happen to those dollars: some leave entirely (churned accounts), some shrink (contraction — downgrades, e.g. the annual → monthly people at ~day 92), and some grow (expansion — upgrades, and here credit top-ups too).

GRR = (B − churned − contraction) / B   ·   NRR = GRR + expansion / B

GRR is what survives of the original money — it can only go down, 100% is its ceiling, and nothing sold to survivors can flatter it. NRR adds survivors' extra spending back and can exceed 100% ("net negative churn": survivors expand faster than leavers churn and the base grows without a single new customer). Measured on billing cycles so renewals don't smear across calendar boundaries; win-backs excluded as recovery. Here NRR peaks at 85% and never reaches the line — which is the quantitative form of "upsell cannot substitute for fixing the first bill."

DEFINITIONS — GROSS LTV: FORMULA, 36-CYCLE HORIZON, WHY NO SURVIVAL MODEL
LTVgross = Σm=0..36 rm · S(m) / (1+d)m

rm — observed revenue per surviving customer in cycle m, subscription + credits, so expansion sits inside LTV. S(m) — the chained renewal curve: each cycle's rate read off the accounts that actually reached it, multiplied up; accounts too young to face a renewal leave the denominator, which handles censoring by construction. d — 20%/yr, applied monthly.

Why cap at 36 cycles: the horizon isn't what the number rests on — the present-value curve is flat well before cycle 36 and 98% of LTV lands within 12 cycles; 36 is a generous ceiling that costs nothing. Discount × horizon together move the answer by $8; the assumed tail churn moves it by $88, so the range ($115–203) is quoted on the tail, not the discount.

Why not fit a survival model on 6 months of data: five observable cycles cannot identify a 36-cycle parametric curve — with clean billing data the empirical chained rates are the Kaplan–Meier estimate, and a fitted tail (shifted-beta-geometric, Weibull) would make the same tail assumption while hiding it inside a distribution choice. The explicit rule instead: read a cycle off the data while it still holds ≥5% of the group; past that, hold revenue flat and decay survival at the mean renewal rate of cycles 2+ (cycle 1 excluded as a different regime — the 49% cliff never repeats). When more data arrives, the honest upgrades are: one more quarter of billing (halves the range), then sBG on 9–12 observed cycles, then per-segment tails.

GROSS LTV BY SEGMENT — spread comes from revenue (6×), not survival (11pp)
4.6× spread → a single blended CAC target overpays for one-shot and underbids for top usage. CAC ceilings per segment.
NET LTV — THE COST CEILING RUNS BACKWARDS (breakeven $/image job, video = 8× image)
Cost per job and CAC are not in the data — a net-LTV figure would be an assumption dressed as a result. What can be computed exactly: the compute cost at which each segment stops paying for itself. 16× spread, reverse order of LTV: if cost/image ≈ $0.01, top usage is the best customer; above ~$0.04 it is sold at a loss while one-shot stays profitable. Get cost per generation before scaling acquisition of heavy users.
DEFINITIONS — NET LTV, THE BREAKEVEN INVERSION, AND THE ~3.5% FEE
LTVnet = Σm [ rm(1−f) − cimg·gimgm − cvid·gvidm ] · S(m)/(1+d)m − CAC

Per cycle: revenue after payment fees (f), minus image-compute cost (cost per image job × image jobs) and video-compute cost, weighted by survival, discounted; CAC off the top. Of the inputs, rm, S(m) and the job counts g are in the data; cimg, cvid and CAC are not — so a net-LTV number would be an assumption dressed as a result.

The inversion: instead of assuming cost and computing profit, set net value to zero and solve for the one unknown — the compute price at which a segment exactly pays for itself:

c*img = [ Σ rm(1−f)·S(m)/(1+d)m ] / [ Σ (gimgm + k·gvidm)·S(m)/(1+d)m ] ,   k = cvid/cimg = 8

Numerator = discounted, survival-weighted lifetime revenue after fees; denominator = the same weighting applied to cost-weighted jobs, with k collapsing two job types into one unit (a video priced as 8 images — the second number for finance to confirm). Below c* a segment is profitable, above it it's sold at a loss — no assumption about the actual cost is needed to compute the threshold. Result: $0.64 / $0.24 / $0.22 / $0.12 / $0.04 — a 16× spread in the reverse order of LTV.

Where ~3.5% comes from: f models payment-processor take (Stripe-tier pricing ≈ 2.9% + $0.30 per charge). On this ticket mix — $14 Basic to $43 Pro monthly, plus credit packs — the fixed 30¢ adds ~0.7–2pp on top of 2.9%, netting out around 3.5% blended. It's called "knowable" because it's a published market rate, not internal data; and at 3.5% it moves every breakeven by under 4%, so it exists for honesty (revenue Higgsfield keeps), not to change a conclusion.

PART 2 · HISTORICAL ANALYSIS

How the segments moved

OPEN: CHECK PLAN MIX BY COHORT

Aggregate ARPU falling has two causes with opposite fixes — mix shift (acquisition brings different people) or within-segment decay (the same people are worth less). Decomposed symmetrically, no interaction term to argue about. The sign flips with the baseline: April is the launch month (1,181 accounts), so anchoring a trend on it manufactures a decline. Honest reading: ARPU roughly flat since May.

The decomposition — symmetric, so mix + value sum exactly to ΔARPU

ΔARPU = Σs (ws¹ − ws⁰) · (vs⁰+vs¹)/2  [mix]  +  Σs (ws⁰+ws¹)/2 · (vs¹ − vs⁰)  [value]

ws = segment share, vs = segment ARPU. Each change is priced at the midpoint of the other variable, so there is no interaction residual to park inconvenient dollars in, and the split doesn't depend on which period is called the base.

ΔARPU APR → AUG, WATERFALL: MIX +$2.28, VALUE −$10.36
Anchored on April, mix actually helps; the whole decline is within-segment value.

One conclusion: the headline's sign depends entirely on whether the launch month is the baseline — so there is no general ARPU decline to fix. What survives both baselines is a single localized story: the heavy segment's share tripled while its per-user value fell 42%, and that is where the pricing question lives.
HABIT-HEAVY: SHARE ×3, ARPU −42% — what both windows agree on
Heavy users arrive in far greater numbers and each is worth less. Two explanations, opposite fixes: self-selection onto cheaper plans (packaging) vs wider acquisition reaching a less committed audience (channel). The $70-vs-$121 gap ≈ one plan tier, which favours packaging — plan mix by cohort settles it; if packaging, reprice the heavy tier.
FIRST RENEWAL BY COHORT — 58% → 48% (±1–3pp)
Cohorts differ on level, not shape — rescaled to their own M1, curves sit within 6–9pp, so the difference is customer quality, not how survivors decay. Consequence for planning: the $117 LTV is a backward-looking average; on August's traffic it comes out lower, and that is the number for CAC decisions today.
